Maier-Saupe Theory of Nematic Liquid Crystals

  • We saw the Landau-de Gennes free energy for nematic liquid crystals. f-f_0=\frac{a}{3}\left(T-T^{\ast}\right) \mathcal{S}^2-\frac{2 b}{27} \mathcal{S}^3+\frac{c}{9} \mathcal{S}^4 .

    • phenomenological (the coefficients are results of a perturbation)
    • based on symmetries only (rotational invariance, head-tail symmetry)
    • no molecular basis

  • The Maier-Saupe theory is a simple alternative microscopic mean-field theory that gives us a more physical insight.

  • The idea is to construct the free energy as we have done in various prior cases by identifying:

    • an entropic contribution
    • an energetic contribution

We need a microscopic description of the orientations:

  • angular deviations \theta from the director and their probability distribution p(\theta)
  • a scalar orientational order parameter \mathcal{S}

Orientational distribution and order parameter

Recall \mathcal{S}=\langle P_2(\cos\theta)\rangle, an average over particles. A microscopic theory needs instead the probability distribution p(\Omega) of finding a rod with polar and azimuthal angles \Omega=(\theta,\phi), with the polar axis along \mathbf{n}.

  • The nematic phase has cylindrical symmetry around \mathbf{n}, so p depends only on \theta: integrating over \phi, we use p(\theta)=2\pi\,p(\Omega).

So, by definition of an average, the orientational order parameter reads

\begin{aligned}\mathcal{S}&=\dfrac{3}{2}\left\langle(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{n})^2-\frac{1}{3}\right\rangle = \dfrac{1}{2}\langle 3 \cos^2\theta-1\rangle=\langle P_2(\cos\theta)\rangle\\ &=\int_0^{\pi}P_2(\cos\theta)p(\theta)\sin\theta d\theta \end{aligned} where we used the Jacobian in spherical coordinates and normalised p(\theta) as \int_0^\pi p(\theta)\sin\theta d\theta=1 (isotropic: p=\frac12).

Two rods. For a rod \mathbf{u} and a rod \mathbf{u}' drawn from p: \big\langle P_2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}')\big\rangle_{\mathbf{u}'}=\mathcal{S}\,P_2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n})

Sketch: \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}'=\cos\theta\cos\theta'+\sin\theta\sin\theta'\cos(\phi-\phi'). Averaging over \phi' removes the cross term and turns \cos^2(\phi-\phi') into \frac12, leaving P_2(\cos\theta)\,P_2(\cos\theta'); averaging over \theta' gives \mathcal{S}.

Energetic Contribution

  • We assume molecules interact with an angular potential that depends on their orientations via the second Legendre polynomial P_2(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(3 x^2-1\right):

V\left(\Omega_i, \Omega_j\right)=-u P_2\left(\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{u}_j\right)

We can average over all possible orientations of molecule j (two-rod average above) to get the mean-field potential felt by molecule i: U_i^{\mathrm{mf}}(\Omega)=\rho \int V\left(\Omega, \Omega^{\prime}\right) p\left(\Omega^{\prime}\right) d \Omega^{\prime}=-u \mathcal{S} P_2(\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{n})

where \mathcal{S} is the order parameter defined above and u is the energetic coupling (a scalar, which absorbs the density \rho; not to be confused with the unit vectors \mathbf{u}_i).

Average over all orientations of molecule i to get the average interaction energy per molecule (and divide by 2 to avoid double counting):

U^{mf} = \frac{1}{2}\big\langle U_i^{\mathrm{mf}}\big\rangle_i = -\frac{u}{2}\,\mathcal{S}\,\big\langle P_2(\mathbf{u}_i\cdot\mathbf{n})\big\rangle_i = -\frac{u}{2} \mathcal{S}^2

A potential that aligns molecules with the director, quadratic to reflect head-tail symmetry.

Entropic contribution

The second contribution is entropic in nature.

Use the orientational distribution p(\theta) introduced above (uniaxial symmetry around the director \mathbf{n}).

The usual Gibbs (or Shannon) entropy functional -k_B \int p(\Omega)\ln p(\Omega) d\Omega contributes to the free energy as

f_{\rm entropy }=k_B T \int_0^\pi p(\theta) \ln (2 p(\theta)) \sin \theta d \theta

where \sin \theta d \theta comes from the solid angle and, with p(\theta)=2\pi p(\Omega), we dropped the constant -k_BT\ln 4\pi: the factor 2 sets the entropy of the isotropic state to zero. 1

  1. Check that for the isotropic distribution p(\theta)=1/2 the entropy term is zero.

Functional derivatives

  • Both \mathcal{S}[p] and f[p] are functionals: they take a whole function p(\theta) and return a number.

  • For a discrete distribution p_i we would write dF=\sum_i \dfrac{\partial F}{\partial p_i}\,dp_i. In the continuum p_i\to p(\theta) and the sum becomes an integral, which defines the functional derivative: \delta F=\int \frac{\delta F}{\delta p(\theta)}\,\delta p(\theta)\,d\theta

  • The basic rule is the continuum version of \partial p_j/\partial p_i=\delta_{ij}: \frac{\delta p(\theta')}{\delta p(\theta)}=\delta(\theta-\theta')

  • Minimising F means \delta F=0 for every small change \delta p, i.e. \dfrac{\delta F}{\delta p(\theta)}=0 at every \theta (plus constraints).

Functional derivatives: two rules

1. Local rule. If F=\int_0^\pi G\big(p(\theta')\big)\,d\theta', differentiate the integrand with respect to p: \frac{\delta F}{\delta p(\theta)}=\frac{\partial G}{\partial p}\Big|_{\theta'=\theta}

  • Entropy: \;f_{\rm entropy}=k_BT\displaystyle\int_0^\pi p(\theta')\ln\big(2p(\theta')\big)\sin\theta'\,d\theta'\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{\delta f_{\rm entropy}}{\delta p(\theta)}=k_BT\left(\ln\big(2p(\theta)\big)+1\right)\sin\theta
  • Order parameter: \;\mathcal{S}=\displaystyle\int_0^\pi P_2(\cos\theta')\,p(\theta')\sin\theta'\,d\theta'\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{\delta \mathcal{S}}{\delta p(\theta)}=P_2(\cos\theta)\sin\theta
  • Normalisation: \;\displaystyle\int_0^\pi p(\theta')\sin\theta'\,d\theta'\;\Rightarrow\;\sin\theta


2. Chain rule. For a function of a functional, such as \mathcal{S}^2: \frac{\delta\, h(\mathcal{S})}{\delta p(\theta)}=h'(\mathcal{S})\,\frac{\delta \mathcal{S}}{\delta p(\theta)} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{\delta \mathcal{S}^2}{\delta p(\theta)}=2\mathcal{S}\,P_2(\cos\theta)\sin\theta

Gradient terms p'(\theta) need an integration by parts (aside).

Maier-Saupe Theory of Nematic Liquid Crystals

Together, we have the Maier-Saupe free energy functional per molecule

f=f_0-u \frac{\mathcal{S}^2}{2}+k_B T \int_0^\pi p(\theta) \ln (2 p(\theta)) \sin \theta d \theta

The only constraint is normalization of p(\theta): \int_0^\pi p(\theta) \sin \theta d \theta=1 where the \sin \theta comes from the solid angle element.

Introduce the Lagrange multiplier \lambda to enforce the constraint (see the aside on Lagrange multipliers) and minimize the functional and set the variation to zero: \delta\left[f+\lambda\left(\int_0^\pi p(\theta) \sin \theta d \theta-1\right)\right]=0 Using the rules above for the constraint term:

\frac{\delta f}{\delta p(\theta)}+\lambda \sin \theta=0

Maier-Saupe Theory of Nematic Liquid Crystals

Remember that - by definition -

\mathcal{S}=\left\langle P_2(\cos \theta)\right\rangle=\int_0^\pi P_2(\cos \theta) p(\theta) \sin \theta d \theta

so, by the chain rule for the energy and the local rule for the entropy,

\dfrac{\delta f}{\delta p} =-u\mathcal{S} P_2(\cos\theta)\sin\theta +k_BT\left(\ln(2 p(\theta)) + 1\right) \sin \theta

and hence, after simplifying \sin \theta, our Lagrange equation reads

-u \mathcal{S} P_2(\cos \theta)+k_B T(\ln (2 p(\theta))+1)+\lambda=0

Its solution yields

p(\theta)=\frac{1}{Z} \exp \left(\frac{u \mathcal{S}}{k_B T} P_2(\cos \theta)\right)

where the partition function Z ensures normalization.

Self-consistency in the Maier-Saupe Result

The solution is said to be self-consistent because p(\theta) depends on \mathcal{S}, which in turn depends on p(\theta).

The self-consistency condition is

\mathcal{S} = \int_0^\pi P_2(\cos \theta) p(\theta) \sin \theta d\theta

where p(\theta) itself depends on \mathcal{S}:

p(\theta) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp\left(\frac{u \mathcal{S}}{k_B T} P_2(\cos \theta)\right)

This equation must be solved for \mathcal{S} at each temperature T.

In general, this requires a numerical solution (see problem classes for a special case where we can make progress analytically).

Quick comparison Maier-Saupe vs Landau-de Gennes

  • Both theories predict a first-order isotropic-nematic phase transition with a discontinuous jump in the order parameter \mathcal{S}.
  • Landau-de Gennes is phenomenological, Maier-Saupe is microscopic.
  • Maier-Saupe provides a molecular basis for the coefficients in Landau-de Gennes
  • Both theories can be extended to include spatial variations in the order parameter and director field, leading to descriptions of defects and elasticity in nematic liquid crystals.
  • Maier-Saupe requires self-consistency requirement, while Landau-de Gennes is often simpler to analyze analytically: (slightly) harder numerics.
  • Easier to cast Maier Saupe onto a lattice model (Lebwohl-Lasher).

Hard rods: ordering from entropy alone

Maier-Saupe needs u\neq 0. But even hard rods, with no attractions at all, go from isotropic to nematic at high density (Onsager, 1949): the transition is purely entropic.

Two entropic contributions compete:

  • orientational entropy favours the isotropic phase: aligning reduces the available orientations
  • translational entropy favours the nematic phase: aligned rods exclude less volume to each other, so each rod has more free volume to move in

At high density the translational gain wins: the nematic phase has the higher total entropy.

Link to Maier-Saupe (Onsager): two rods exclude most volume when crossed, least when aligned. Averaged over orientations, the excluded-volume term in f/k_BT contains -\rho L^2D\,\mathcal{S}^2, up to a number (rods of length L, diameter D): the Maier-Saupe form, with the density \rho L^2D playing the role of u/k_BT.

Note

Same story as the freezing of hard spheres: the spheres lose the disorder of their positions, but each gains free volume to vibrate around its lattice site.

Aside (optional): simple lattice model, Lebwohl-Lasher

In 2D, \frac32\cos^2\Delta\theta-\frac12=\frac34\cos2\Delta\theta+\frac14: the model is the XY model (planar spins) for the angle 2\theta.

  • no true long-range order at T>0 (Mermin–Wagner, below)
  • at low T, orientational correlations decay as a power law (quasi-long-range order) rather than exponentially
  • the transition happens when bound \pm\tfrac12 defect pairs unbind (Kosterlitz–Thouless; defects later today)

(Mermin-Wagner theorem) Continuous symmetries cannot be spontaneously broken at finite temperature in systems with sufficiently short-range interactions in dimensions d \leq 2.

Here the continuous symmetry is the rotational invariance of the director field in the plane.

Continuum limit and Frank elasticity

Charles Frank (1958, of our Frank lecture theatre) developed a continuum theory to describe the elastic properties of nematic liquid crystals.

The Frank free energy density describes the cost of spatial variations in the director field \mathbf{n}(\mathbf{r}):

f_{\text{Frank}} = \frac{1}{2} K_1 (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{n})^2 + \frac{1}{2} K_2 (\mathbf{n} \cdot \nabla \times \mathbf{n})^2 + \frac{1}{2} K_3 (\mathbf{n} \times \nabla \times \mathbf{n})^2

  • K_1: splay elastic constant
  • K_2: twist elastic constant
  • K_3: bend elastic constant

Pure splay, twist and bend compared to equilibrium from Binder et al. J. Phys. Mater. (2020)

With this free energy, one can model large-scale deformations and defects in nematic liquid crystals.

Topological Defects in Nematic Liquid Crystals

  • We are working in the continuum with a director field \mathbf{n}(\mathbf{r}) that describes the local average orientation of molecules.

  • In this framework, defects are singularities of the field, called disclinations

  • Examples in 2D:

  • These singularities cannot be removed by any smooth deformation of the field: they are topologically protected.

  • To make them vanish, one needs oppositely signed defects to bring together so that they annihilate each other.

  • At finite temperature, defects are spontaneously created/annihilated.

Topological Charge of a Defect

  • The topological charge (or winding number) s of a defect quantifies how many times the director field rotates by 2\pi when encircling the defect once.

\Delta \theta=2 \pi s

For example, a charge of s=1/2 is a rotation by \pi is performed when going around the defect once.

Aside (optional): conservation of topological charge

  • Topological charge is conserved: the total charge in a closed system remains constant over time.
  • Defects can be created or annihilated only in pairs of opposite charge (e.g., +1/2 and -1/2).
  • The topology of the space imposes such constraints of conservation via the Poincaré-Hopf theorem, which states

The total topological charge of the defects in a field confined to a compact space is equal to the Euler characteristic of that space

In practice:

  • sphere (\chi=2): total charge +2 (e.g. four +\tfrac12)

  • torus (\chi=0): total charge 0, as in periodic boxes

  • disc (\chi=1), director tangent to the edge: total charge +1

  • This imposes constraints on the dynamics and interactions of defects.

Director fields on a sphere, on a torus and a double torus. The number of handles (the genus g) determines the Euler characteristic \chi=2-2g. From Nelson (2002)

Many physics problems can be cast in terms of constrained optimisation

Area Problem Constraint
Mechanics Minimize potential energy of a pendulum Fixed length of the string
Thermodynamics Maximize entropy S Fixed total energy E
Statistical Mechanics Maximize Gibbs entropy -\sum_i p_i\ln p_i Normalisation \sum_i p_i=1, fixed \langle E\rangle

Liquid Crystals Minimize Maier-Saupe free energy f[p] Normalisation of p(\theta)

Method: Use Lagrange multipliers \lambda to convert constrained into unconstrained problem: \mathcal{L}=f(\mathbf{x})-\lambda g(\mathbf{x}) then solve \nabla\mathcal{L}=0.

Aside (optional): geometrical understanding of Lagrange multipliers

  • Take a function f(x,y).
  • A constraint is expressed by fixing the value of a second function g(x,y)=c
  • We want to find the extrema of f(x,y) subject to the constraint g(x,y)=c: It has to be a curve
  • This means find the extremal values of f on the curve \mathcal{C}: g(x,y)=c
  • We could parametrise \mathcal{C} but we can also simply think in terms of its geometrical features.

Aside (optional): geometrical understanding of Lagrange multipliers

Take the example of a minimum:

  • at every point of \mathcal{C} there is a tangent vector \vec{t}
  • we want to find the points on \mathcal{C} where f is minimal. This means that changes of f along \mathcal{C} are minimal.
  • we can calculate such changes as the projection of the local gradient with the tangent vector and state that \vec{\nabla}f \perp \vec{t}
  • Notice that, by definition, \vec{\nabla}g\perp \vec{t}
  • So we are simply saying that

\vec{\nabla}f\parallel \vec{\nabla}g\to \vec{\nabla}f=\lambda\vec{\nabla}g

for some scalar \lambda.

Aside (optional): many variables, example of probabilities

Discrete case: Find extremum of entropy S = -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i with constraints \sum_i p_i = 1 \quad \sum_i p_i E_i = \langle E \rangle

Using Lagrange multipliers: \mathcal{L} = -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i - \lambda_1 \left(\sum_i p_i - 1\right) - \lambda_2 \left(\sum_i p_i E_i - \langle E \rangle\right)

Setting \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p_i} = 0: -\ln p_i - 1 - \lambda_1 - \lambda_2 E_i = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad p_i = \exp(-\lambda_2 E_i - \lambda_1 - 1)

This is the Boltzmann distribution: p_i \propto \exp(-\beta E_i) with \beta = \lambda_2.

Aside (optional): functional derivatives with gradient terms

  • When the functional depends also on the gradient \phi', as for an action functional S[\phi] (that you may have already seen), the rules of the main slides need an integration by parts:

S[\phi] = \int \ell(\phi(x), \phi'(x), x) dx

\ell is the Lagrangian density: e.g. x is space, \phi(x) a field defined over space (e.g. density)

  • Perturb S[\phi] by an amount \epsilon with an arbitrary function \eta(x) vanishing at the boundaries

S[\phi+\epsilon\eta]=\int \ell(\phi+\epsilon\eta, \phi'+\epsilon\eta', x) dx

Expanding to first order in \epsilon:

S[\phi+\epsilon\eta]\approx S[\phi]+\epsilon\int\left[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi}\eta+\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi'}\eta'\right] dx

Aside (optional): functional derivatives with gradient terms

Integrating by parts the second term:

\int\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi'}\eta' dx = \left[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi'}\eta\right]_{\text{boundary}}^{\phantom{x}}-\int\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi'}\right)\eta dx

Since \eta(x) vanishes at the boundaries, the first term drops out, giving:

\delta S=\int\left[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi}-\frac{d}{d x}\left(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi^{\prime}}\right)\right] \eta(x) d x

If we want S[\phi] to be stationary with respect to arbitrary perturbations \eta(x), then \delta S=0 for all \eta(x).

This leads to the Euler-Lagrange equation:

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi}-\frac{d}{d x}\left(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi^{\prime}}\right)=0

which defines the functional derivative as \frac{\delta S}{\delta \phi(x)}=\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi}-\frac{d}{d x}\left(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi^{\prime}}\right)

Aside (optional): continuous limit

Replace sums with integrals. Let p_i \to p(x) where x is a continuous variable.

Constraints become: \int p(x) dx = 1, \quad \int p(x) E(x) dx = \langle E \rangle

Entropy functional: S[p] = -\int p(x) \ln p(x) dx

Lagrangian: \mathcal{L} = -\int p(x) \ln p(x) dx - \lambda_1 \left(\int p(x) dx - 1\right) - \lambda_2 \left(\int p(x) E(x) dx - \langle E \rangle\right)

Functional derivative \frac{\delta \mathcal{L}}{\delta p(x)} = 0: -\ln p(x) - 1 - \lambda_1 - \lambda_2 E(x) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad p(x) = \exp(-\beta E(x) - c)
